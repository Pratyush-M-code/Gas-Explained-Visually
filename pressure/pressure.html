Pressure

Introduction to Pressure

Pressure is usually defined as force by area of application.
But where does that pressure come from? In gases, pressure arises from the collisions of the gas particles with the walls of the container. These collisions occur due to the repulsive force of these particles in relation to one another!

The 3D model on the right is made of balls (representing particles) that are packed tightly at one end and spread out at the other end.
You can drag to rotate it!
When the balls are crowded, they exert more pressure on their surroundings! This is what high pressure represents: greater density of particles. While on the other end, the particles are further spaced. Low pressure represents a lower density of particles.

Pressure in Gases

Let's take the example of the right-hand-side wall!
The pressure felt on this wall is due to the collisions of the gaseous particles (the ones that are black) with the wall. Since the simulation is 2D, the area upon which the force is applied will be taken to be the side length.

Every bounce of a black particle adds to the force on that wall. The readout averages the hits over about one second, because a single hit is far too brief to measure. In real life, there are so many particles, and they are so spread out and randomized that the pressure is averaged out to be constant.

Average Pressure

The graph shows the recording of the instantaneous pressure, and the dashed line is the average of all the readings. Single values fluctuate, but the average remains constant.

Pressure on the Whole Box

Now, we'll record all the four walls taking hits. Each wall has its own colour: red on the left, blue on the right, yellow on top and green on the bottom. A particle is coloured on the basis of the wall it is heading toward, so when two particles collide and change direction, their colours can change too.

Every wall adds up its own hits. Add the force on all four walls and divide by the total length of the walls, and you get the pressure of the whole box:
